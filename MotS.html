How the orbits become sound & sources

The conversion: one orbit becomes one cycle, so f = 1 / (P × 86,400), with P in days. Multiplying by 2 repeatedly raises that cycle by exact octaves. Radiation frequencies are lowered by dividing by 2. No equal-tempered scale, random detuning, vibrato, or pitch rounding is applied.

Sidereal / native uses the source’s stated physical cycle: fixed-star orbital periods for the Solar System, a galactic or stellar orbit for deep-sky entries, and a radiation-spectrum peak for the CMB. The different quantities are labeled explicitly. Tropical uses a mean equinox-based model: 1/Pₜ = 1/Pₛ + q, where q = 1/365.242190402 − 1/365.256363004 cycles/day. This is a constant J2000 precession approximation, not a full precession or perturbation model. It does not mean each planet's own seasonal year.

Synodic uses 1/S = |1/P − 1/Pᵣ|, with Pᵣ set by your selected reference planet or asteroid. This describes the difference in mean heliocentric orbital rates. It is an approximate alignment clock, not a full prediction of the apparent sky from that location; eccentricity, inclination, perturbations, and light travel are omitted. A body compared with itself is silent. The Moon’s familiar solar phase cycle uses its geocentric sidereal period minus Earth’s heliocentric rate, and is available with Earth selected. A lunar cycle seen from another planet needs a separate geometric model, so this version marks it unavailable. Rotation is not mixed with orbital motion.

Octave folding shifts each source independently into 128–256 Hz, keeping frequency ratios only up to powers of two. One shared time scale applies the same multiplier to all bodies, preserving their full frequency ratios. Tones outside 20–12,000 Hz are omitted and marked in the table. Sources spanning very different time scales will not all fit the listening range under one common multiplier.

Orbit choir is the most direct listening mode: steady sine waves at the calculated frequencies, initially dry. Celestial pianist adds a recurring eight-bar arrangement and a gentle harmonic timbre to the same fundamentals. Rhythm, loudness, room sound, visual layout, and poetry are artistic choices. These are sonified cycles, not acoustic recordings of planets through space.

The instrument keeps ten audio slots. Each slot can use a planet, the Moon, one of your seven asteroids, or a supported deep-sky source. Pluto and Ceres are dwarf planets. The full original catalog is retained below for reference; old assigned tones are not silently treated as measured orbital frequencies. Planetary periods are mean values; asteroid periods are JPL orbit snapshots with their own stated epochs. This small offline model does not reproduce eccentric-orbit speed changes, all gravitational perturbations, Doppler shifts, or present sky positions. Rings are a schematic of relative cycle rates, with a separate visual time scale of one Earth sidereal year per minute. A radiation source has a stationary marker. Mixed deep-sky rings indicate separate clock sources and are not drawn as if they orbit one common center. Radii are laid out for readability, and zero phase means the start of the listening session.

Data sources, checked 30 September 2026: NASA / JPL planetary periods (Julian years × 365.25); IERS / Paris Observatory constants (Earth sidereal and tropical years, lunar mean motion); US Naval Observatory definitions. Published precision limits the model; extra displayed digits show the calculation, not extra measurement accuracy.

Asteroid data: Ceres, Vesta, Pallas, Juno, Eros, Apophis, and Bennu use the NASA/JPL Small-Body Database orbital-period fields retrieved 30 September 2026. Their orbit epochs are displayed in the voice details and preserved in the file. These are fixed snapshots, including Bennu’s older solution epoch; the app does not update them online or model future close encounters.

Deep sky: “Sun → Galactic center” uses an approximate 230-million-year galactic orbit (NASA). “S2 → Sagittarius A*” uses an approximate 16-year stellar orbit (ESO). Neither is a single frequency belonging to the black hole or Galactic center itself.

CMB spectrum peak: this is the cosmic microwave background, the relic radiation in your original catalog. It is not a material edge of space. The marker uses an approximate 160.2 GHz peak in spectral radiance per unit frequency, shifted down 30 octaves to about 149.198 Hz (KIAS; NASA/COBE). The CMB has a broad spectrum; this single marker does not reproduce its entire spectrum. Sidereal, tropical, and synodic orbital corrections do not apply to electromagnetic frequency. A CMB rest-frame / Doppler mode would require a different model.

Great Attractor: your original entry is preserved in the catalog. It is a region of concentrated mass associated with large-scale gravitational flows (NASA/Hubble). This version has no established periodic cycle to assign it. Its old 73.41 Hz value is labeled as a legacy assignment, and is not offered as a measured orbital tone. Sonifying a velocity field or density map would be a separate, explicitly defined mapping.

Pause holds your place. Stop resets the performance. New phrase reshapes the pianist's arrangement without changing orbital pitches. Solo buttons isolate one body; press again to restore the mix. Your controls are saved locally when storage is available. A sleep timer starts with play and counts elapsed time, including pauses.

Keyboard: Space play / pause · S stop · N new phrase · M mute · F full screen. Shortcuts are disabled while you use a form control.

Everything runs offline. Economy visuals default to 20 fps and a 1× canvas for older phones such as the Samsung S9. Audio uses no more than ten simultaneous oscillators. Background playback still depends on the browser and operating system; if audio is suspended, return and press Resume. Keeping the screen awake requires browser support and may be unavailable for a local file.
